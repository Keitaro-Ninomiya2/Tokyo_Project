\subsection{Worker Reallocation in Response to Military Drafting}

Table~\ref{tab:reallocation} reports OLS estimates of how offices respond to losing male workers to military drafting. The unit of observation is position $\times$ kakari $\times$ year (1938--1945). Column~1 shows that each additional draft in the own position raises total transfers in by approximately 0.15 workers ($\beta = 0.147$, $p < 0.05$). Table~\ref{tab:transfer-decomposition} decomposes these transfers by organizational distance: same-ka transfers within the same kyoku are negligible ($-0.02$, insignificant), while transfers from a different ka within the same kyoku increase by 0.29 workers per draft ($p < 0.01$) and transfers from a different kyoku increase by 0.15 workers ($p < 0.05$). This indicates that the reallocation response operates primarily across sections rather than within them. Table~\ref{tab:reallocation-newhires} reports new hiring. Column~1 shows that each draft raises new hiring by approximately 0.22 workers. All specifications include year, ka, and position fixed effects; standard errors are clustered at the office level.

These results indicate that offices relied on both internal reallocation and external hiring to replace drafted workers, but the two channels operated at different organizational scales. Internal transfers were concentrated at the cross-section level, consistent with a centralized human resource team reassigning workers across ka units within the same department. The near-zero within-ka response suggests that adjacent kakari did not directly exchange workers in response to drafting, and that the reallocation was instead coordinated at a higher organizational level.

\subsection{Gender Composition of the Hiring Response}

Table~\ref{tab:reallocation-newhires} investigates whether drafting-induced vacancies are filled differently by gender. Columns~4--6 report OLS estimates for female new hires: the coefficient is small and insignificant (0.007). Columns~7--9 report male new hires: each draft increases male hiring by 0.21 workers. Table~\ref{tab:gender-share} reports OLS estimates for female share of the workforce and female share of new hires by rank (R1 = temporary, R2 = regular). The coefficients on the any-drafted indicator are small and statistically insignificant across all four specifications (female share of all workers at R1 and R2, and female share of new hires at R1 and R2). This indicates that male hiring increases in both absolute and proportional terms, while female hiring does not systematically respond to drafting, leaving the gender composition of the workforce and of new hires unchanged.

The absence of a gender composition effect distinguishes the drafting-induced hiring response from the broader wartime trend of rising female employment documented in Section~\ref{sec:women-timeseries}. While the aggregate female workforce grew substantially during the war, this growth was not driven by the direct replacement of drafted men. Instead, the external labor market supplied predominantly male replacements to drafted offices, and the entry of women into the civil service appears to have been associated with voluntary male exits and the general expansion of wartime administration.

\subsection{Short-Run Promotion in Response to Drafting}

Table~\ref{tab:promotion} reports OLS estimates of how military drafting affects the promotion probability of non-drafted coworkers. The dependent variable is an indicator equal to one if a worker's rank increased from the prior year (linear probability model). The unit of observation is individual non-drafted workers in consecutive years. Column~1 shows that each additional draft from the same office increases the probability of promotion by 8.8 percentage points ($\beta = 0.088$, $p < 0.05$). Column~2 adds a gender interaction: the female interaction is positive and significant (2.8 percentage points, $p < 0.01$), indicating that women in drafted offices experienced a larger increase in promotion probability than men. Column~3 interacts with rank: the effect is concentrated among rank~2 workers (\emph{shoki/gite}), with no significant differential for rank~1 (\emph{yatoi}) or rank~3 (\emph{shuji/gishi}) positions. Column~4 interacts with an engineer indicator: engineers experience a significantly smaller promotion response ($-4.3$ percentage points, $p < 0.01$), consistent with engineering roles having more rigid promotion criteria. All specifications include year, ka, and position fixed effects; standard errors are clustered at the office level.

The promotion response reveals an important mechanism through which offices absorbed the loss of drafted workers: rather than relying solely on external replacement, managers advanced existing lower-ranked staff to fill vacated roles. The larger effect for women suggests that drafting may have loosened barriers to female advancement, at least in the short run. The muted response among engineers is consistent with technical positions requiring specialized credentials that limited the scope for internal promotion.

\subsection{Bonus Salary Response to Drafting}

Table~\ref{tab:rinji} reports OLS estimates of how military drafting affects the number of workers receiving bonus salary (\emph{rinji tokubetsu}) allowances. The unit of observation is position $\times$ office in the 1944 cross-section. Column~1 shows that each additional draft increases the number of workers on bonus salary schedules by 0.14 units ($\beta = 0.136$, $p < 0.05$). Column~2 adds rank interactions: the effect is concentrated among rank~2 workers (0.154, $p < 0.01$), while rank~1 (\emph{yatoi}) workers show a significantly smaller response ($-0.18$, $p < 0.01$). Column~3 interacts with an engineer indicator: the bonus response is significantly larger for engineers (0.15, $p < 0.05$), suggesting that offices used compensation to retain workers with specialized skills. Column~4 interacts with the position's female employment share; the interaction is negative but imprecise. All specifications include ka and position fixed effects; standard errors are clustered at the office level.

Together with the promotion results, these findings suggest that offices responded to drafting through a combination of internal career advancement and financial incentives. The concentration of bonus allowances among rank~2 workers and engineers indicates that offices targeted retention efforts at the staff most difficult to replace through external hiring, consistent with firm-specific human capital being a key consideration in the replacement strategy.

\subsection{Retention of Non-Drafted Workers}

Table~\ref{tab:retention} reports OLS estimates of how military drafting affects the retention of non-drafted coworkers. The dependent variable is an indicator equal to one if the worker appears in the directory in the following year. Column~1 shows that each additional draft is associated with a 0.9 percentage point increase in retention probability, but the estimate is not statistically significant ($p > 0.10$). Column~2 adds a gender interaction: the female interaction is negligible (0.2 percentage points, insignificant). Column~3 adds a rank interaction: the rank~1 interaction is negative ($-0.9$ percentage points) but insignificant. Column~4 interacts with an engineer indicator: the engineer interaction is similarly small and insignificant. All specifications include year, ka, and position fixed effects; standard errors are clustered at the office level.

The lack of a significant retention effect suggests that while offices used promotions and bonus salaries to manage the disruption caused by drafting, these measures did not detectably reduce turnover among remaining staff. This is consistent with retention decisions being driven primarily by factors outside the office's control, such as external labor market conditions and further rounds of conscription.

\subsection{Postwar Outcomes of 1944 Colleagues}

Table~\ref{tab:future} examines postwar outcomes (1946--1955) among non-drafted workers observed in 1944. Columns~1--3 report OLS estimates with z-scored rank change as the dependent variable (postwar average rank minus 1944 rank, standardized within era). The coefficient on the any-drafted indicator (column~1) is 0.058 (approximately 0.06 standard deviations) but imprecise. Column~2 uses draft share instead; the coefficient is 0.073 and similarly imprecise. Column~3 adds a gender interaction: the main effect of draft share remains positive (0.055), the female main effect is negative ($-0.57$), and the draft share $\times$ female interaction is large and positive (1.06), suggesting that women in more heavily drafted offices may have experienced larger rank gains than men, though the interaction is not statistically significant. Columns~4--5 examine postwar retention (count of years appearing in civil service through 1955): the coefficients on any drafted and draft share are positive (0.037 and 0.050) but imprecise. Columns~6--7 analyze the average female share of workers' destination office-positions in the postwar period; drafting exposure does not systematically predict the gender composition of postwar placements (coefficients near zero and insignificant). All specifications include ka and position fixed effects from 1944; standard errors are clustered at the office level.

The imprecise but consistently positive point estimates for rank advancement and retention suggest that wartime drafting may have modestly accelerated the careers of non-drafted colleagues, though the effects are too noisy to draw firm conclusions. The null result on destination female share indicates that the wartime experience of working alongside women in a drafted office did not systematically alter the gender composition of workers' postwar placements at the individual level.

\subsection{Effect of Drafting on Office Size}

Table~\ref{tab:headcount} reports OLS estimates of how military drafting affects remaining office headcount. The unit of observation is kakari $\times$ position $\times$ year (1938--1945). The dependent variable is the number of non-drafted workers remaining in the office. Column~1 shows that each additional male drafted increases remaining headcount by 0.92 workers ($p < 0.01$). Column~2 reveals substantial heterogeneity by rank: the effect is larger for rank~3 (\emph{shuji/gishi}) positions (8.55 additional workers, $p < 0.01$) and significantly smaller for rank~1 (\emph{yatoi}) positions ($-2.16$, $p < 0.01$). All specifications include ka, position, and year fixed effects; standard errors are clustered at the office level.

The positive coefficient may appear counterintuitive but reflects the fact that offices experiencing drafts were simultaneously receiving transfers and new hires to compensate for the loss. The net effect on remaining headcount thus captures both the mechanical reduction from drafting and the replacement hiring documented in the preceding tables. The large rank~3 effect suggests that offices with drafted senior workers attracted disproportionately more replacements, consistent with greater organizational effort to fill higher-ranked vacancies.

\subsection{Characteristics of Donor Offices}

Table~\ref{tab:donor} compares offices that transferred workers out against those that did not, conditional on ka $\times$ position fixed effects. The table reports differences in whether the office hired anyone new that year, average tenure length, and female share. These comparisons shed light on whether offices selected to surrender workers had distinct workforce characteristics, offering a test of vacancy-chain theories.

Panel~A shows that donor offices are unconditionally more likely to have hired new workers (77\% vs.\ 61\%, $p = 0.03$) and have significantly more new hires on average (15.9 vs.\ 5.3, $p < 0.01$). However, average tenure and female share are indistinguishable between donor and non-donor offices. Panel~B conditions on ka, position, and year fixed effects: the hiring differential remains marginally significant (12 percentage points, $p = 0.085$), while tenure and female share differences vanish entirely. The conditional hiring difference suggests that offices selected as transfer sources tended to be larger or have higher baseline turnover, but were not systematically different in workforce composition. Importantly, the absence of additional hiring at donor offices conditional on organizational structure is inconsistent with a simple vacancy-chain model, in which poaching from one office should trigger replacement hiring at the source.

\subsection{Characteristics of Transferred Workers}

Table~\ref{tab:transferred_chars} examines the individual characteristics of workers who were transferred out of donor offices. The regressions test whether transferred workers were disproportionately female, had different tenure lengths, or held different ranks (as a proxy for productivity), conditional on ka $\times$ position fixed effects.

Table~\ref{tab:donor_postwar} examines postwar outcomes (1946--1955) for workers who remained in donor offices compared to workers in adjacent kakari that did not experience transfers. Workers in donor offices experienced significantly lower postwar rank advancement ($-0.31$ standard deviations, $p < 0.01$), while postwar tenure was slightly lower but insignificant ($-0.24$, $p > 0.10$). The gender interaction in column~2 indicates that women in donor offices experienced an additional tenure penalty ($-0.33$ years, $p < 0.10$).

The transferred worker characteristics in Table~\ref{tab:transferred_chars} reveal that selection into transfer was not systematically related to gender, tenure, or rank: all three coefficients are small and insignificant. This suggests that the human resource team did not selectively transfer lower-quality workers, but instead reassigned workers based on the organizational needs of the receiving office. The negative postwar rank outcomes for workers remaining in donor offices may reflect the loss of experienced colleagues whose departure reduced opportunities for mentorship or collaborative advancement.

\subsection{Women in the Civil Service, 1928--1955}

Figure~\ref{fig:timeseries-women} plots the number of women employed in the Tokyo civil service from 1928 to 1955, distinguishing between \emph{kanri} (appointed officials) and \emph{kouri} (public employees). Female participation was effectively zero before 1937 and rose sharply during the war years as offices responded to labor shortages caused by military drafting. The postwar trajectory shows whether wartime gains in female employment persisted through the reform period. \label{sec:women-timeseries}

The aggregate pattern reveals that the wartime entry of women into the civil service was not fully reversed after the war. While total female employment declined from its wartime peak, the postwar level remained substantially higher than the pre-war baseline, particularly among \emph{kouri} employees. This persistence is notable given the return of demobilized soldiers and the broader reversion of wartime labor market conditions, and motivates the analysis in the following subsections of whether individual managers' wartime exposure to female coworkers influenced their subsequent staffing decisions.

\subsection{Assignment of Women to Offices Post-Reform}

Table~\ref{tab:exposure-manager} reports OLS estimates linking post-reform female inclusion under a manager to the manager's wartime exposure to female coworkers. Columns 1--3 model the probability that a manager includes at least one woman. A 10 percentage point increase in exposure raises this probability by 4.2 points using the maximum exposure measure (column 1) and by 7.2 points using the mean exposure measure (column 2). Column 3 restricts the sample to departmental managers (kacho); the estimate is positive but imprecise. Columns 4--6 report the extensive margin: a 10 percentage point increase in exposure is associated with 0.1--0.2 additional women in the manager's office unit, depending on the measure. The first-stage relationship and IV results are discussed in the next subsection.

\subsection{The Causal Effect of Exposure on Post-Reform Female Assignment}

Table~\ref{tab:iv-control-function} compares OLS and IV estimates of how wartime exposure to female coworkers affects the post-reform assignment of women to offices. The OLS coefficient is positive and significant, consistent with Table~\ref{tab:exposure-manager}. The IV estimate (Panel A, column 2) points in the same direction and is larger in magnitude (5.03 vs.\ 1.73) but only marginally significant ($p < 0.10$), with a weak first-stage F-statistic (3.19). Columns 3--4 restrict to managers with wartime exposure; the IV estimate remains positive but the first-stage strength collapses (F = 0.07). The results do not allow rejection of a zero causal effect; interpretation must emphasize limited statistical power rather than a precise null.

\subsection{Network Proximity and Transfer Selection}

To investigate whether organizational reallocation follows existing workplace networks, we construct a measure of past co-worker connections between donor and destination offices. For each worker observed in year $t{-}1$, we check whether any colleague in their current office previously shared a workplace (office $\times$ ka) with anyone currently working at an office that experienced military drafting in year $t$. We define ``self-connected'' workers as those who themselves have such a past link. Table~\ref{tab:network} reports the results.

Panel~A of Table~\ref{tab:network} reports the informativeness of this network measure. We document the share of workers for whom the connection measure is non-zero, which is critical for interpreting the regression results: if the measure is zero for nearly all workers, we cannot distinguish between null effects and an underpowered measure. Panel~B reports OLS estimates of whether past connections predict transfer probability, conditional on year, ka, and position fixed effects. Column~1 tests whether working in an office with any past connection to a drafted destination predicts being transferred. Column~2 tests the individual-level measure: whether a worker who themselves previously co-worked with destination office members is more likely to be transferred. Column~3 includes both measures simultaneously to distinguish office-level from individual-level selection. Column~4 uses the intensive margin (number of connected colleagues). Column~5 interacts the self-connection measure with gender.

These results shed light on the mechanism underlying the reallocation response documented in Table~\ref{tab:reallocation}. If transfers are coordinated through personal networks---as in referral-based hiring---we would expect positive coefficients on the connection measures. If instead transfers are centrally assigned by the personnel office without regard to prior workplace ties, the coefficients should be small and insignificant.

\subsection{Long-Run Outcomes of Transferred Workers}

Table~\ref{tab:transfer-outcomes} compares the long-run career outcomes of transferred workers against two natural reference groups: peers at the destination office (Panel~A) and peers who remained at the donor office (Panel~B). The dependent variables capture tenure after transfer, postwar survival in the civil service (appearing in any year 1947--1955), number of postwar years observed, and rank advancement (maximum rank achieved minus rank at transfer).

Panel~A conditions on destination office $\times$ transfer year fixed effects, so the comparison isolates whether transferred workers fare differently from the incumbents they joined. Panel~B conditions on donor office $\times$ transfer year fixed effects, comparing transferred workers to the colleagues they left behind. The key question is whether wartime transfers conferred a career advantage or disadvantage: did the disruption of being moved accelerate or hinder career progression relative to staying in place?

\subsection{Post-War Retention of Wartime Hires}

Table~\ref{tab:postwar-iv} examines whether workers hired during the wartime labor shortage persisted in the civil service after the war ended. We identify 21,410 wartime new hires (workers whose first appearance falls in 1940--1945), of whom 1,993 were female and 19,417 male. The overall postwar retention rate---defined as appearing in any year between 1947 and 1955---is 15.6\%. Strikingly, the female retention rate is substantially lower at 7.6\%, compared to 16.5\% for males.

Panel~A reports the first stage: the relationship between office-level draft intensity and new hiring. The coefficient on the number of males drafted is positive but imprecise (0.11, SE 0.21), indicating that draft intensity is a weak instrument for hiring at the office level. This reflects the fact that hiring decisions are driven by many factors beyond the immediate draft vacancy, including administrative expansion and budget allocation.

Panel~B reports reduced-form estimates of draft intensity on postwar outcomes. While the share of wartime hires retained or promoted shows no significant relationship with draft intensity, the number of workers promoted postwar is significantly \emph{lower} in offices with greater wartime drafting ($-0.064$, $p < 0.05$). This suggests that workers hired into heavily drafted offices may have been negatively selected or that these offices experienced greater postwar reorganization.

For the gender-specific analysis, we restrict the female sample to low-rank administrative positions (rank~1--2), as these are the positions most sensitive to the drafting shock. The first stage for female low-rank hires is also weak (coefficient 0.024, SE 0.034), reflecting the small number of female hires in any given office. The reduced-form retention estimate for women is positive (0.007) but imprecise. The IV estimates, presented in Panel~C, are correspondingly noisy due to the weak first stage and should be interpreted with caution.

\subsection{Comparison with Related Literature}

Our findings contribute to and extend several strands of the literature on worker substitutability, wartime labor market disruptions, and the persistence of female employment gains.

\paragraph{Worker substitutability and replacement costs.} \citet{JaegerDeath} analyze 34,000 unexpected worker deaths in Germany and find that coworker earnings increase by approximately 0.6\% in the year following the death, with cumulative effects of roughly 6,000 EUR (18\% of the deceased worker's annual earnings) over five years. The positive wage effects are concentrated among same-occupation coworkers, while workers in different occupations experience wage \emph{decreases} when a high-skilled worker or manager dies. Our context differs in that the exiting workers are drafted rather than deceased, and the organization responds through a combination of internal transfers, new hiring, and internal promotion rather than solely through wage adjustments. Our finding that offices respond to drafts with an 8.8 percentage point increase in promotion probability for remaining workers is consistent with J\"ager et al.'s evidence that firms use internal labor market mechanisms to adjust to worker exits. However, while J\"ager et al.\ find positive wage effects and increased retention, we find null retention effects and instead document a promotion-and-bonus response. This difference likely reflects the institutional context: in the rigid salary schedules of the Japanese civil service, promotions and bonus allowances (\emph{rinji tokubetsu}) served the role that wage adjustments play in private German firms.

Both studies confirm that worker exits create substantial adjustment costs and that incumbents and outsiders are imperfect substitutes. J\"ager et al.\ find that these effects are larger in thin external labor markets; analogously, our finding that offices rely heavily on cross-section internal transfers (Table~\ref{tab:transfer-decomposition}) rather than external hiring suggests that the wartime civil service operated in a thin market where replacement costs were high.

\paragraph{Female labor supply and wartime mobilization.} \citet{Acemoglu} exploit cross-state variation in male mobilization rates during World War~II and find that a 10 percent increase in female labor supply reduced female wages by 7--11\% and male wages by 4--6\%. At midcentury, women were closer substitutes for high-school-educated men than for those with lower or higher skills. \citet{101221} demonstrate that the wartime impact on female labor supply persisted through 1950 and 1960, particularly among women in the top half of the education distribution.

Our setting provides a complementary perspective from a non-Western bureaucratic context. Unlike the U.S., where wartime female labor supply increases were substantial and persistent, our data show that the \emph{direct} replacement of drafted men was predominantly male---female hiring did not respond to office-level drafting (Table~\ref{tab:reallocation-newhires}). The aggregate rise in female civil service employment documented in Figure~\ref{fig:timeseries-women} was thus not driven by the direct replacement channel but rather by the general expansion of wartime administration and voluntary male exits. This finding challenges the standard narrative, derived primarily from U.S.\ evidence, that wartime labor shortages mechanically drew women into male-dominated occupations.

Nevertheless, consistent with \citet{Acemoglu} and \citet{101221}, the wartime gains in female employment were not fully reversed. Our postwar retention analysis shows that 7.6\% of wartime female hires remained in the civil service through 1947--1955, and the aggregate time series confirms persistently higher female representation. The lower female retention rate compared to males (7.6\% vs.\ 16.5\%) is consistent with the pattern documented by \citet{Rose_2018}, who shows that much of the wartime female labor force participation in the U.S.\ was reversed by 1950, with persistence concentrated among older married women.

\paragraph{Wartime wage data.} Unlike the U.S.\ context studied by \citet{GoldinMargoQJE} and \citet{Acemoglu}, where detailed wage data allow estimation of the wage effects of female labor supply shocks, the Japanese civil service operated under centralized salary schedules that limit within-position wage variation. Our data record salary levels but not the detailed pay grades within positions. Japan's Basic Survey on Wage Structure (\emph{Chingin K\=oz\=o Kihon T\=okei Ch\=osa}) began in 1948 and reports gender-disaggregated wages from that year onward, but historical data from the wartime period covering public sector employees by gender are not available in published form. The female-to-male wage ratio in Japan was approximately 60\% in the late 1980s and has risen to about 72\% by 2014, but comparable figures for the 1940s civil service cannot be constructed from available sources. Our analysis of bonus salary (\emph{rinji}) allowances in Table~\ref{tab:rinji} provides the closest available proxy for compensation responses to labor shortages.

\paragraph{Persistence and the transmission of gender norms.} \citet{NBERw32639} study how the expansion of female clerical employment in the U.S.\ federal government during World War~I persistently shifted gender norms, finding intergenerational effects on daughters' labor supply and earnings. Our manager-exposure results (Tables~\ref{tab:exposure-manager} and \ref{tab:iv-control-function}) document a related but distinct channel: managers who worked alongside women during the war were more likely to integrate women into their postwar offices. While our IV estimates lack power due to a weak first stage, the consistent positive OLS relationship suggests that direct workplace exposure may have shifted managers' willingness to hire women, complementing the intergenerational transmission channel documented by Aneja, Farina, and Xu.
