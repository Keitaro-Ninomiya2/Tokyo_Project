We study how large organizations adapt to exits of staff due to military drafting. 
Despite a vast literature documenting how mobilization of workers from workplaces accelerated turnover (\cite{Goldin1991WWII}, \cite{Acemoglu}, \cite{Ferrara}), little is known about how internal labor markets shaped which positions became available for replacement workers.
Theories of internal labor markets describe how workplaces respond to exits by retaining current workers through promotions and salary raises (\cite{LazearRosen1981}, \cite{JaegerDeath}).
The ability of workplaces to find replacements within their workforce should 
determine which positions and occupations become available to external workers, as well as the implications for the overall integration of under-represented groups into the workforce \citep{NBERw32639}.

We document the occupational and office-unit responses to fill drafting-induced vacancies through both internal and external labor markets of a large public sector employer. 
We construct a novel database on civil servants in wartime Tokyo, which provides the positional information of the universe of its employees as well as a list of conscripted workers.
The national government of Japan kept its military drafting process hidden from eligible male workers; drafted workers were therefore assigned to a wide range of ranks and office units upon conscription\footnote{1,422 of 1,924 position--office cells across four years.}. 
We control for the eligibility status of the workers by exploiting the  multi-layered hierarchical structure of the Tokyo civil service, and conditioning our estimates on office-by-occupation fixed effects.
We examine how offices where draftees worked before conscription responded through (1) retaining existing staff by offering promotions and salary increases, (2) transferring workers from distant office units, and (3) seeking replacements from external labor markets.

Under our conditional exogeneity design, supported by pre-war balance checks within section (\emph{ka}) and position cells, we find that each additional draft-induced exit generated about twice as many internal transfers (0.41) as external hires (0.22).
The response is driven mainly by transfers from a different section within the same department (about 0.29 workers) and from different departments (about 0.14 workers).
Alongside recruiting workers from internal and external sources, offices mitigated the disruption caused by drafting by promoting workers in lower-ranked positions and offering bonus salaries to the remaining staff to retain them.
A one-unit increase in the number of drafted workers increased the probability of a non-drafted coworker being promoted by 8.7 percentage points.
An additional draft also increased the number of existing staff receiving bonus salary (\emph{rinji tokubetsu}) allowances by 0.14 units.
We find that the retention probability for existing workers was slightly higher in draft-impacted offices (0.9 percentage points per draft), but the estimate is statistically insignificant.

Descriptive comparisons suggest that the central HR team sourced transferees from large offices with sufficient organizational slack to absorb the loss of a worker. Conditional on bureau, position, year fixed effects, and log headcount, donor offices had about 3.9 more new hires per year and 0.12 years longer average tenure than non-donor offices---consistent with offices that combined a stable core workforce with high entry-level flow. Within donor offices, selection into transfer was not predicted by the worker's gender, tenure, or rank---all three coefficients are small and statistically insignificant. The hiring gap between donor and non-donor offices predates the transfer itself, with 5.1 additional hires in the prior year, consistent with pre-existing organizational capacity rather than vacancy-chain hiring in response to the outbound transfer.

Transferred workers had better subsequent career outcomes than peers at both destination and origin offices: 10.5 and 16.3 percentage point higher postwar survival rates, 0.12 and 0.18 units greater rank advancement, and 1.7 and 3.4 additional years of tenure, respectively. Workers who remained in donor offices experienced 0.31 standard deviations lower postwar rank advancement relative to peers in same-section non-donor offices. Observable attributes---gender, tenure, and rank---do not predict selection into transfer within donor offices, though we cannot rule out selection on unobservables. The outcome gaps are consistent with organizational position mattering for career progression, but we interpret these comparisons as descriptive. Draft-induced transfers and external hires were predominantly male, leaving no statistically significant change in office gender composition; each additional draft reduced average tenure in the affected office by 0.07 years. A reduced-form test finds no relationship between the wartime draft shock in a manager's office and female inclusion in that manager's postwar office, conditional on wartime section and position.

The results underscore how workplace disruptions due to military drafting were met by workplaces seeking replacements from internal sources rather than from the external market.
The expertise of workers constitutes a valuable asset for workplaces, and replacing workers with external hires imposes an additional cost (\cite{doi:10.1086/258715}, \cite{JaegerDeath}).
The composition of the neighboring organizational structure predicts the success of finding a replacement to fill the role, as the central human resource management team can identify departments with unused employees and relocate them to the impacted office.
In contrast to the large literature arguing that draft-induced vacancies drew women into male-dominated workplaces \citep{Goldin1991WWII, Acemoglu}, in our setting the inflow of women into the civil service was mostly associated with voluntary male exits amid an overheated wartime labor market rather than direct replacement of drafted workers.
