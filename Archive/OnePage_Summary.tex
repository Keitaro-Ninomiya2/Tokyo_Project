{\normalsize \textbf{One-Page Summary: Wartime Drafting and Bureaucratic Labor Reallocation in Tokyo}}

\vspace{0.3em}
\textbf{1) Research Question}
\begin{itemize}
    \setlength\itemsep{0pt}
    \item How does a large bureaucracy replace workers lost to military conscription?
    \item Is adjustment primarily internal reallocation or external hiring?
    \item Do draft shocks increase female representation?
\end{itemize}

\textbf{2) Approach}
\begin{itemize}
    \setlength\itemsep{0pt}
    \item Novel administrative panel of wartime Tokyo civil servants across offices, positions, and years.
    \item Office--position draft exposure regressions with section, position, and year fixed effects, validated by pre-war balance checks.
    \item Tracks transfers, hires, promotions, bonus pay, retention, and postwar careers.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{3) Findings}
\begin{itemize}
    \setlength\itemsep{0pt}
    \item Per draft exit: about \textbf{0.41 internal transfers} vs \textbf{0.22 new hires}; moves are mostly across sections/bureaus.
    \item Offices also promote incumbents (\textasciitilde8.7 pp per draft) and expand bonus allowances to retain staff.
    \item Replacement is largely male; draft exposure does not significantly raise office female share.
    \item Transferees have better later retention/advancement, but this is descriptive (non-random selection may remain).
\end{itemize}

\textbf{4) Contributions}
\begin{itemize}
    \setlength\itemsep{0pt}
    \item Provides rare micro-level evidence on wartime public-sector internal labor markets.
    \item Shows centralized internal redeployment is the dominant replacement margin.
    \item Nuances wartime gender narratives: female entry reflects broader labor-demand expansion more than direct draft substitution.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{5) Future Directions}
\begin{itemize}
    \setlength\itemsep{0pt}
    \item Compare these draft-driven staffing responses against manager displacement caused by military bombing during the Tokyo air raids.
\end{itemize}
