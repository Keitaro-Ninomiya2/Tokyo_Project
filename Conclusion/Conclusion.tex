This paper examines how organizations respond to exogenous labor supply shocks and how these responses shape long-run workforce composition, leveraging a unique historical shock: the mass conscription of male workers during World War II in the Tokyo civil service. Using a novel personnel dataset covering 92,000 workers from 1928 to 1958, we trace the full chain of organizational responses---from immediate reallocation through postwar persistence.

Our findings reveal several key insights. First, offices responded to drafting through a combination of internal transfers (0.15 additional workers per draft, concentrated at the cross-section level), new hiring (predominantly male), and internal promotion of remaining workers (8.8 percentage points per draft). The reliance on cross-section rather than within-team transfers, combined with the use of bonus salary allowances to retain scarce workers, points to a centralized human resource function managing the reallocation.

Second, the direct replacement of drafted men was overwhelmingly male: female hiring did not respond to office-level drafting, and the gender composition of affected offices remained unchanged. The aggregate wartime increase in female civil service employment was instead associated with the general expansion of wartime administration rather than the mechanical replacement of drafted workers. This finding challenges the standard narrative from the U.S.\ literature that wartime labor shortages directly drew women into male-dominated occupations.

Third, we examine the long-run consequences of wartime disruptions. Wartime hires had a postwar retention rate of only 15.6\%, with women retained at roughly half the male rate (7.6\% vs.\ 16.5\%). At the manager level, wartime exposure to female coworkers predicted greater postwar female integration, though IV estimates lack statistical power. The persistence of female employment gains despite low individual retention rates suggests that the wartime experience normalized female participation at the organizational level.

By documenting the complete organizational response to labor supply shocks---from immediate replacement through long-run persistence---this study contributes to the literatures on worker substitutability \citep{JaegerDeath}, wartime female labor supply \citep{Acemoglu, 101221}, and the transmission of gender norms \citep{NBERw32639}. Our results highlight that the mechanisms of persistence operate differently in a bureaucratic context with centralized personnel management than in the decentralized private-sector labor markets studied in prior work.
